# one-on-one-notes-synthesizer

## Overview
Clusters raw 1:1 notes or transcripts spanning a review cycle into recurring
themes — growth, blockers, wins, sentiment, and open commitments — and produces
a short prep brief for the next conversation. Its one job is turning a pile of
dated notes the manager already has into the *pattern* across them, not
restating what is already on the page.

## When to use
- A manager has a backlog of dated 1:1 notes or transcripts and needs a
  synthesized view ahead of the next check-in.
- Prepping accomplishment or growth-area evidence to feed into a performance
  review or calibration packet.
- Checking whether a recurring blocker or a promised action item has actually
  been resolved across several sessions, rather than re-reading every note.
- File/artifact patterns: a folder or document of dated 1:1 notes, meeting
  transcripts, or chat-log exports from recurring check-ins.

## Workflow
1. **Treat the input as sensitive personal data from the first line.** These
   notes typically contain career concerns, performance worries, health or
   personal disclosures, and compensation discussion. Do not persist the
   synthesis beyond the working scope of the requested task, do not use it for
   any purpose beyond what was asked, and strip identifying detail from any
   output that will be shared more broadly than the original notes were.

2. **Ingest and preserve chronology.** Parse the raw notes into dated sessions.
   Chronological order is what makes recurrence detection possible in the next
   step — do not flatten everything into one undated bucket.

3. **Extract into fixed theme buckets** for each session:
   - *Growth* — skill development, stretch opportunities, career-goal talk.
   - *Blockers* — recurring impediments, escalations needed, friction points.
   - *Wins* — accomplishments, positive feedback received or given.
   - *Sentiment/morale signals* — anything indicating engagement, burnout risk,
     or satisfaction, stated plainly rather than over-interpreted.
   - *Action items/commitments* — what either party said they would do, and by
     when.

4. **Detect recurrence, not just presence.** An item mentioned once is a
   data point; an item mentioned across three or more consecutive sessions is a
   pattern worth surfacing on its own line, with the date span attached (for
   example: *"Raised in the last four sessions (May 3–Jun 14): unclear
   ownership on the migration work"*). Do not bury a persistent pattern inside
   a single-session bullet list.

5. **Track open commitments across sessions.** For each action item logged,
   check later sessions for evidence it was addressed. Report status as
   open / closed / stale (raised, never mentioned again, and evidently
   unresolved) rather than silently dropping ones no one revisited.

6. **Draft a prep brief, not a re-transcription.** The manager already has the
   raw notes; the value-add is the synthesis. Keep the brief short: a handful of
   themes, the persistent items, the open commitments, and two or three
   suggested talking points for the next conversation. Quote directly only when
   the exact wording matters for accuracy — otherwise summarize.

7. **Flag anything ambiguous rather than guessing at intent.** If a note is
   terse or the sentiment is unclear, say so instead of inferring a conclusion
   the raw text does not support — this is feedback material for a real
   relationship, not a place to editorialize.

## Checklist / quality gate
- [ ] Output is derived only from the supplied notes — no invented details or assumed context.
- [ ] Themes are separated cleanly into growth / blockers / wins / sentiment / action items.
- [ ] Recurring items are called out explicitly with a session count and date span, not just listed once.
- [ ] Every open commitment has a status: open, closed, or stale.
- [ ] The brief is a synthesis, not a re-transcription of notes the manager already holds.
- [ ] Sensitive content is not persisted or forwarded beyond the immediate task's scope.
- [ ] A grammar pass has been run on the final brief before handoff.
